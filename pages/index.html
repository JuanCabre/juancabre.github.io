About me

I understand communication networks as infrastructure for communication, storage, and computing. This idea has shaped my work on coded transport, coded storage, and coded computing since my doctorate. Now, networks are moving even further. Their nodes sense and act, and some of them are embodied AI agents: robots and machines that run learned models and work among people. Together with the sensors, machines, and infrastructure around them, they form physical AI.

It is within this context that I do my research. My central hypothesis is that the task should become the unit by which networks are designed and operated, as the packet and the flow are today. A task specifies the output it needs, its deadline, and the error it tolerates, and from this the network can determine what to communicate, what to store, and where to compute. I want these networks to be sovereign, trustworthy, and sustainable.

Two lines of work carry this program. In post-Shannon communication, the network delivers what the task requires instead of every bit. In network coding, one code can protect information both in transit and in storage. I care about the path from theory to systems, so much of this work ends in an emulator, a testbed, or a demonstrator.

I grew up in Venezuela, where I studied electrical engineering at Universidad Simón Bolívar. I moved to Denmark for an M.Sc. in wireless communication systems at Aalborg University, and in 2015 I joined TU Dresden, where I received my doctorate in 2022.

FFoundations

Networks that communicate, store, and compute

How do we design communication and computing together?

I approach networks as programmable infrastructure in which communication and computing are designed together. Software-defined networking, network function virtualization, and network coding can be combined in the mobile edge cloud to meet the latency and resilience requirements of the Tactile Internet. To test such ideas on commodity hardware, I helped build and evaluate ComNetsEmu, an open-source emulator for software-defined and virtualized networks, and I have taught its use in several IEEE tutorials together with Professors Frank H. P. Fitzek and Fabrizio Granelli.

1Direction 1

Maintaining state across transport and storage

How does a network keep the state a task requires available under failures, mobility, and updates?

Storage is communication across time, so the codes that make transport reliable can also keep stored information available. On the transport side, PACE engineers the redundancy of random linear network codes so that coded packets meet low-latency targets. SpaRec keeps recoding sparse and systematic at intermediate nodes of multi-hop networks. OneCode uses a single random linear network code to generate packets, recode them over several hops, and repair the redundancy that is lost when nodes fail. In related work, we study peers at the network edge that cache network-coded content and leave or fail over time, and we define a measure, the grade, of how far the coded cache has degraded. For embodied agents, this state is what they have sensed and learned: maps, the memory of past tasks, and model checkpoints.

2Direction 2

Communicating to coordinate distributed tasks

What must nodes exchange to coordinate a task correctly and on time?

Between AI agents, the question extends from representing information to organizing interactions: who asks whom, in what order, and by which deadline.

Task-oriented communication: functional and semantic compression

Does a receiver need all the data to perform its task?

In 6G-life, I proposed practical functional compression for networked control as a research direction and guided the two doctoral researchers who developed it. Functional compression sends what the receiver needs to compute a function of the data, instead of the data itself. For networked extended reality, we compress point clouds semantically: cXR+ conveys them as voxel-based color codes, and AIcXR+ uses a spiking neural network to shorten latency at about the same compression ratio. The ball-balancing robot demonstration is under Demonstrators.

Identification codes

In message identification, the receiver only checks whether one event of interest occurred, which requires far fewer bits than transmitting the message. We proposed performance metrics for identification codes that are closer to practical systems than the asymptotic bounds, and we wrote a tutorial that opens these codes to communication engineers outside information theory. Together with the Chair of Communication Networks at TU Munich, we applied them to a truck platoon whose vehicles send short tags to verify their local control decisions against an edge server at the base station. In simulation, this hybrid scheme reduced resource consumption at least fourfold compared to fully centralized control.

Function computation and networked coordination

Can nodes test whether they agree without exchanging their full states?

In 6G-life, together with Holger Boche's group at TU Munich, we studied this question for relay networks. I contributed the systems perspective and co-developed the link between message identification and the computation of a function chosen by the receiver. The same line of work led to a German patent on communication devices and methods for message identification.

3Direction 3

Executing and recovering reliable distributed computation

Where should computation run, and how should it recover as resources and connections change?

Where computation runs matters, because processors differ widely in speed and in the energy they need for the same task. In the gAIn project, I supervised the doctoral researcher who benchmarked edge AI accelerators against two general-purpose GPUs: an in-memory accelerator reached a throughput between those of the two GPUs at about 6 W, compared to 82 W for the faster GPU. In MLcXR+, with Nokia, we studied where the compression load runs on 5G edge application servers: on independent servers, or on servers chained as successive compression levels.

Placing computation across robots and servers with these costs in mind remains open, and the MUSE demonstrator below is a first step. Recovery concerns the task and not only the service: restarting a failed node may not suffice if others have already acted on its results. My formal starting point is coded computing, which adds redundancy to computation as coding adds it to transport and storage. A German patent from this line describes a selector that chooses a computing substrate according to the requirements of the task.

Demonstrators#

Much of this work ends in a demonstrator. I guided and supervised the work behind the ones below. In the two most recent, shown at IEEE CSCN 2026 in London, visitors trigger the events themselves.

3Direction 3

@@PUBTITLE:nazari2026muse@@

IEEE CSCN 2026, London, demonstration

Drones on a simulated factory floor offload tasks to two edge servers and a cloud. When a visitor triggers an event on a tablet, a slow loop with a language model (LLM) raises the urgency of the affected task, and deterministic checks validate its output. A fast reinforcement-learning (RL) scheduler then decides where each task runs.

The language model never allocates resources, and at 0.3 s to 3.8 s per update it stays outside the time-critical loop.

2Direction 2

@@PUBTITLE:nazari2026semantic@@

IEEE CSCN 2026, London, demonstration

On a simulated factory floor, drones, vehicles, robotic arms, and human operators send state updates over real TSN hardware. Visitors steer an entity with a joystick and compare the ground truth with the monitored view. When a drone approaches a person, its updates get more frequent and urgent, while the transmission schedule stays fixed.

For now, thresholds on freshness and safety relevance set each flow's priority; learning this policy is future work.

@@PUBAUTHORS:nazari2026semantic@@
2Direction 2 Best Demo Award

@@PUBTITLE:rezwan2024balancing@@

IEEE CCNC 2024, Las Vegas, demonstration

A robot uses a camera and two motors to balance a ball on a plate, while an edge server runs the vision computation. With functional compression, the server returns only a compact color code. Visitors throw the ball and move the computation between servers at six locations, from Stockholm to Melbourne, while the robot keeps balancing.

Depending on the scheme, the code stands for ball position or motor voltages, cutting transmitted data by 70% to 90%.
