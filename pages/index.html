I want to understand how we must design networks so that humans and embodied AI agents can coexist and collaborate in the world they share.

I understand communication networks as infrastructure that transports, stores, and computes information. When I started my doctorate, this was a new way to look at networks, and it has shaped my work on coded transport, coded storage, and coded computing ever since. Now, networks are moving even further. Their nodes sense and act, and some of them are embodied AI agents: robots and machines that run learned models and work among people. This is what is known as physical AI.

Every node in such a network has some capability to sense, compute, store, and act, and these capabilities differ widely. In a network with many users, what do we have to store, communicate, and compute, where, and when? My thesis is that the task should become the unit by which networks are designed and operated, as the packet and the flow are today. A task states the output it needs, its deadline, and the error it tolerates, and from this the network can derive what to communicate, what to store, and where to compute.

I want these networks to be sovereign, trustworthy, and sustainable. Sovereign means that owners decide where their data and models reside and run. Trustworthy means, first of all, dependable: under crashes, losses, and delays, a task meets its deadline, or the system says that it cannot, and a late answer never causes an accident. Sustainable means less energy per completed task.

For most of its history, communication engineering has worked on one problem, which Shannon described as that of "reproducing at one point either exactly or approximately a message selected at another point." His framework made that problem tractable, and it still carries the Internet today. However, many of the applications that networks now serve do not need the message itself. A robot needs a control decision. A digital twin needs to know whether one particular event occurred. A group of agents needs to know whether they agree.

Two tools carry much of my work. I work on post-Shannon communication, where the network delivers what the task requires instead of every bit, and on network coding, where one code can protect information both in transit and in storage. I care about the path from theory to systems, so much of this work ends in an emulator, a testbed, or a demonstrator.

Since networks compute as well as communicate, I am also interested in coded distributed computing, reliable distributed computing, low latency, and in-network computing. Computing itself is changing as well. Beyond the digital substrate, I want to understand what becomes possible with new computing substrates such as analog, biological, and quantum computing.

I grew up in Venezuela, where I studied electrical engineering at Universidad Simón Bolívar. I moved to Denmark for an M.Sc. in wireless communication systems at Aalborg University, and in 2015 I joined TU Dresden, where I received my doctorate in 2022. Today I lead a junior research group at CeTI and coordinate its research area on computing and communication.

Research#

Three research directions and the foundations they build on, each with the papers that best show it. Journal articles are listed first. The complete list is on the publications page.

Foundations

Networks that communicate, store, and compute

What changes when a network can compute?

These two papers show how I approach networks: as programmable infrastructure in which communication and computing are designed together. In the first, I describe how software-defined networking, network function virtualization, and network coding can be combined in the mobile edge cloud to meet the latency and resilience requirements of the Tactile Internet. The second presents ComNetsEmu, an open-source emulator for software-defined and virtualized networks that runs on commodity hardware. I helped build and evaluate it, and it remains the platform on which we test mechanisms before they reach hardware.

Direction 1

Maintaining state across transport and storage

How does a network keep the state a task needs available when nodes fail, move, or update?

Storage is communication across time, so the codes that make transport reliable can also keep stored information available. OneCode uses a single random linear network code to generate packets, recode them over several hops, and repair the redundancy that is lost when nodes fail. It results from our DFG SPP 2378 project "One Code to Rule Them All," and its evaluation rests on analysis and simulation. The second paper studies peers at the network edge that cache network-coded content and leave or fail over time. We define a measure, the grade, of how far the coded cache has degraded, and use it to decide when to refresh the cache. I worked closely with the two doctoral researchers who led this line of work. For embodied agents, the state to maintain is what they have sensed and learned, such as maps and model checkpoints; this is where I take the work next.

Direction 2

Communicating to coordinate distributed tasks

What must nodes exchange to coordinate a task correctly and on time?

Does a receiver need the whole message to perform its task? In message identification, it only checks whether one event of interest occurred, which requires far fewer bits than transmitting the message. With the doctoral researcher I guided in this line, we proposed performance metrics for identification codes that are closer to practical systems than the asymptotic bounds, and wrote a tutorial that opens these codes to communication engineers outside information theory. With the Chair of Communication Networks at TU Munich, we applied them to a truck platoon whose vehicles send short tags to verify their local control decisions against an edge server; in simulation, this hybrid scheme needed about four times less traffic than fully centralized control.

Functional compression goes one step further: MLcXR+ merges the views of several cameras at edge servers through multilevel semantic compression, in collaboration with Nokia, evaluated with an open-source prototype and a simulated network. With Holger Boche's group at TU Munich, we asked whether nodes can test that they agree without exchanging their full states; I contributed the systems perspective, and Johannes Rosenberger led the proofs.

Direction 3

Executing and recovering distributed computation

Where should computation run, and how should it recover when resources and connections change?

Faster execution on one processor can be outweighed by transfers, queues, migration, and recovery, and processors differ widely in the energy they need for the same task. With Sifat Rezwan, we compare an in-memory AI processing unit with TPU, NPU, and GPU systems-on-chip for AI inference at the edge, in performance and in energy. Measurements like these are what a placement decision needs when it weighs a battery-powered robot against an edge server. The MUSE demonstrator shows the placement side of this direction.

Demonstrators#

We test ideas from all three directions in demonstrators that people can interact with.

Direction 3

@@PUBTITLE:nazari2026muse@@

IEEE CSCN 2026, London · Demonstration

A fleet of drones on a simulated factory floor offloads four kinds of tasks to two edge servers and a cloud. A slow loop, in which a world model and a language model interpret events such as a person approaching the entrance, raises the urgency of the affected task; a fast reinforcement-learning scheduler then decides where each task runs. The language model never controls the drones or the resources directly, and deterministic checks validate its output. Visitors trigger events from a tablet and watch the urgent task move to the faster edge server.

The demo compares four open models: larger models missed fewer urgent tasks, but the slow loop took longer, up to 3.84 s for a reasoning model.

Direction 2

@@PUBTITLE:nazari2026semantic@@

IEEE CSCN 2026, London · Demonstration

State updates of drones, vehicles, robotic arms, and human operators from a simulated factory floor travel over real time-sensitive networking hardware. Each flow's priority and update interval adapt at run time to the age of its information and to its safety relevance: when a drone approaches a person, its updates become more frequent and more urgent, while the network's transmission schedule stays fixed. Visitors steer an entity with a joystick and compare the monitored view with the ground truth.
